# Direct User Interview: Blind Participant

## Purpose and scope

This document records the direct interview with a blind/low-vision participant. It is separate from `InterviewReport.tex`, which documents the October 17, 2026 proxy interview with P1, a former staff member of Deafblind Community Services who was not blind or deaf.

**Source and wording note:** The question wording below follows the 12-question interview guide. Participant responses are paraphrased from the interview summary preserved in the project conversation. They are not verbatim quotations. Each response is lightly expanded for readability, using only details already in that summary. Question 10 was skipped in the interview notes; no answer or finding is inferred for it.

## Interview questions and participant responses

### 1. Tell me a bit about yourself and how technology fits into your day. As much as you’re comfortable sharing, how does your disability affect how you use a computer, and has that changed over time?

The participant had recently graduated and described technology as an integral part of everyday life. AI and audio features help reduce the amount of reading required. In other words, audio and AI features are a regular way for the participant to take in information instead of reading it on a screen.

### 2. Could you walk me through what tasks you might typically use a computer for?

The participant described using technology for directions and travel, researching restaurants and places to visit, and school or learning-related activities. Their technology use covers both everyday planning and their studies.

### 3. What accessibility tools or features or voice assistants do you use most often, and what do they do?

The participant identified zoom and audio features as accessibility supports. Zoom enlarges what is on the screen, while audio features let them listen to information instead of reading it.

### 4. How have these tools been working for you?

The participant described these features as convenient, particularly when glasses or other tools are unavailable. They also described being able to ask their phone to perform tasks. When their usual visual aids are not at hand, asking their phone gives them another way to get things done.

### 5. Walk me through how you tell your computer what to do, for example opening an app, clicking a button, or typing a message.

The participant described using Siri to perform tasks hands-free. This lets them get tasks done by speaking to Siri, without using their hands to operate the device.

### 6. Think about the last time you had difficulty completing something on a computer. What were you trying to do, and what happened?

The participant described a two-step verification problem in which verification codes were not arriving. They contacted IT support to resolve the issue. This was a multi-step security process, and they needed outside help to get past the problem.

### 7. If you need help with a computer task, such as completing an action or setting up a tool, what do you usually do?

The participant described using ChatGPT for help with technical problems and sharing screenshots to show what was happening. Sharing screenshots lets the AI see the problem directly, alongside the participant's description of it.

### 8. When you’re doing a task, how does your computer let you know what’s happening, like whether it understood you, what’s on the screen, or whether something worked?

The participant described AI providing audible responses, checking whether it had understood correctly, and asking for information to be repeated when needed. This feedback tells them whether their request was understood and when they need to repeat it.

### 9. Think about the last time you did something online that mattered to you, such as sending an important email, submitting a form, or making a purchase. How did you make sure it was right before you finished?

The participant described checking outcomes through trial and error. One example involved refreshing an email app and checking for pop-ups or error messages to determine whether something had gone wrong. They confirmed the result by looking for signs in the app itself, rather than assuming the action had worked.

### 10. Can you tell me about a time a website, app, or accessibility tool did something unexpected while you were using it? What happened, and how did you fix it?

This question was skipped or left unanswered in the available interview summary.

### 11. Are there any things you would like to do on a computer that you currently avoid or have someone else do? Why?

The participant described avoiding storing personal information and passwords on the computer. The available summary does not record a specific task they avoid having someone else do. No reason for avoiding this was recorded, so none is assumed here.

### 12. Is there anything else you would like to share that would be important about understanding your experience using a computer?

The participant described AI as useful for simplifying searches. Their closing point was that AI helps them find information with less effort.

## Preliminary design implications

The following are team interpretations of the responses, not additional participant statements:

- Support hands-free interaction and provide clear spoken feedback about what the system understood.
- Make clarification and correction straightforward when speech or a task is misunderstood.
- Help users troubleshoot multi-step authentication problems without implying that the system can bypass security controls.
- Verify consequential task outcomes using observable evidence instead of assuming that an action succeeded.
- Minimize retention of passwords and other sensitive personal information.
- Consider screenshot-based context as a useful troubleshooting input, while communicating uncertainty about what can be inferred from an image.
- Reduce the reading burden where possible, while allowing users to choose the accessibility and feedback format that works for them.
