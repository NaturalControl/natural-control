# Interview: Deafblind Community Services (DBCS)

- **Date:** October 17, 2026
- **Interviewer:** Aaron Loh (Group 7, Natural Control)
- **Participant:** P1, a former special events coordinator at Deafblind Community Services (DBCS), a CNIB organization. P1 is not blind or deaf; they speak from working with DBCS clients.
- **Format:** Conversation transcribed by Aaron.
- **Editing:** Lightly edited for readability. Filler words, false starts, and repeated phrases were removed, and long questions were shortened to the question actually asked. The participant's answers were not reworded beyond that, and nothing was added. Square brackets mark editor notes.
- **Anonymity:** Published without the participant's name. Their name is withheld until they confirm they are comfortable being named.

**Aaron:** Thank you for agreeing to do this short interview for our capstone project. To start with some context: what was your role at DBCS, and who were the people you supported? Were they fully blind, low vision, or deafblind, and what was the age range?

**P1:** I was a special events coordinator at Deafblind Community Services, which provided different kinds of events and services to people who are newly diagnosed with deafblindness. It's more often acquired, not something people are typically born with, so that population is in their later years. It can be earlier, but the people I mostly interacted with were older people in the community.

Our services were non-profit and government-funded, so they were free services for people who generally have low to no income. We provided different types of resources to help them navigate their home tech and other services in the community, and we provided intervenors so they could get access to the things they need. If they were going to the grocery store, an intervenor would come and assist them and provide any kind of visual context, ASL [transcribed as "ALS"; confirm], or hand-touch language.

**Aaron:** What tools do they use that you know of, such as VoiceOver, JAWS, NVDA, or Be My Eyes? From what you could tell, what were the limitations of those tools?

**P1:** For any type of screen reader, I think in that population, who already have some struggles with high-tech devices, it can be complicated. It should be oversimplified so they're able to use it. Some people probably still struggle with it, not only with using the software or having it work, but with navigating it. Also, I don't think many software programs are built with the deaf or the blind in mind. When I'm making something, I'm not thinking about how easy it is for a blind person to navigate.

**Aaron:** Are there any specific times you can recall seeing someone get stuck?

**P1:** No.

**Aaron:** If someone could just say what they wanted done, which tasks would matter most: email, calendar, forms, booking appointments, shopping, banking? This could be for the people you worked with, or even for you.

**P1:** All of the above. Scheduling, formulating emails. Those are everyday things, and they're the kinds of things that population typically looks for help with. Making appointments, they might typically need help with, like having a friend do it.

**Aaron:** What kind of tasks do you think people would not want AI to do for them?

**P1:** I'm sure there are confidentiality concerns. If there are certain healthcare problems, maybe they don't always want to disclose that to the AI. Maybe they just want to book an appointment rather than explain what is wrong. Banking could also be confidential, if they don't want to put all their banking information into an AI and ask it to move money.

**Aaron:** Imagine you're only talking to this assistant by voice, not looking at anything. Before it submits, deletes, or buys something, what would you want to hear first? Would you want it to confirm before it does anything?

**P1:** I'm neither deaf nor blind, so it's hard to say specifically. When I'm driving and can't look at what I'm typing, having Siri read back what I want to send is nice. The only downfall is that if it messes up, you have to cancel the whole message. It would be cool to have it maybe read back sentence by sentence. Confirmation that it heard you correctly.

**Aaron:** How much spoken feedback would you want? Say you asked it to send a message, put something on your calendar, or submit a grocery order. Would you want progress updates, or would you rather it just do it without saying much?

**P1:** I don't think I'd be good at answering that. I can't answer that.

**Aaron:** Fair. That might be better to ask someone who is actually blind, so I may ask you to connect me with someone.

**Aaron:** If it made a mistake, how would someone want to find out and fix it?

**P1:** Would it tell me that a mistake was made, or would I tell it?

**Aaron:** [Attribution uncertain in the notes.] Sometimes it might do something and then realize it messed up.

**P1:** It should tell you, I'm assuming.

**Aaron:** Sometimes. That question isn't working well, so let's skip it. [Question skipped.]

**Aaron:** Who usually sets up new technology for the people you worked with: family, or staff like you?

**P1:** Typically family, but there are also intervenors. Sometimes clients will dedicate some of their time with an intervenor to setting things up.

**Aaron:** How much time would someone spend with an intervenor?

**P1:** It's typically an hour to an hour and a half per day, but it varies. For some people it's a weekly occurrence at least. It's up to the client.

**Aaron:** Where do people typically use their computers: at home, the library, a cafe? Is there any difference for them?

**P1:** If anything, it might be an older device rather than a new MacBook, but I would say more at home, for comfort. It'll usually be a quieter, private place.

**Aaron:** Is there anything I haven't asked that you think matters for building an AI personal assistant like this for blind users?

**P1:** Probably just keeping it as simple as possible, because most of the time these software programs are hard to navigate, and people will skip over things that are complicated to use [transcribed as "We'll skip over"; confirm]. Sometimes, if the screen reader is reading the whole screen, it's reading irrelevant things rather than the things that actually need to be read.

**Aaron:** [Attribution uncertain in the notes.] So what I'm hearing is that it should read the things that matter, and only those, the things that actually have to do with getting the task done.

**P1:** Detailed but simple, if that makes sense.

**Aaron:** Detailed in what ways?

**P1:** Only in the ways that matter.

**Aaron:** Could you introduce us to two or three people who would be willing to try what we build and give feedback?

**P1:** Yeah, probably.

**Aaron:** We'll be in touch about that. Thank you for your time.
